# App-store policy and regulatory risk for peptide / GLP-1 tracker apps (as of Nov 2026)

Method note: research was done by web search and fetches on 27 Nov 2026. fda.gov, support.google.com, 9to5mac.com and lynkdose.com could not be fetched from this session (DNS blocked), so the FDA and Google Play findings rest on search-result summaries of those pages and on secondary sources (law firms, trade press, trackers). Apple's guidelines were fetched directly. Where sources disagree, both versions are given below.

## 1. Apple App Store and Google Play policies, and documented rejections or removals (2024–2026)

### Takeaway
The main app-store risk for a peptide or GLP-1 app is the **dose or reconstitution calculator**. Apple Guideline 1.4.2 allows drug dosage calculators only from a manufacturer, hospital, university, insurer, pharmacy or other "approved entity", or with FDA or equivalent approval. In 2025–2026 several peptide tracker apps removed their calculators to stay listed. Other risks follow: 1.4.1 (medical claims and AI health advice), 5.1.1(ix) (healthcare apps must be submitted by a legal entity), 1.4.3 (sale of controlled substances), and Google Play's ban on promoting or selling "unapproved substances" plus its mandatory health declaration.

### Cited Findings
**Apple (verbatim from the current guidelines page)**
- 1.4.1: "Medical apps that could provide inaccurate data or information, or that could be used for diagnosing or treating patients may be reviewed with greater scrutiny." Apps "must clearly disclose data and methodology to support accuracy claims relating to health measurements… if the level of accuracy or methodology cannot be validated, we will reject your app." Apps "should remind users to check with a doctor… before making medical decisions." If an app has regulatory clearance, the developer should submit a link to the documentation. — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 1.4.1 also bans apps that claim to measure blood pressure, blood glucose, blood oxygen and similar values "using only the sensors on the device". This matters for any rPPG/camera vitals feature. — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 1.4.2: "Drug dosage calculators must come from the drug manufacturer, a hospital, university, health insurance company, pharmacy or other approved entity, or receive approval by the FDA or one of its international counterparts. Given the potential harm to patients, we need to be sure that the app will be supported and updated over the long term." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 1.4.3: apps that "encourage consumption of… illegal drugs" are not permitted. "Facilitating the sale of controlled substances (except for licensed pharmacies…)" is not allowed. — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.1(ix): apps in highly regulated fields, healthcare included, "should be submitted by a legal entity that provides the services, and not by an individual developer." — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.3: health data may not be used or disclosed for advertising or data mining. Apps may not write false data into HealthKit or store personal health information in iCloud. Health research requires consent and approval from an ethics board. — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.2(i): developers must disclose sharing personal data "with third-party AI" and obtain explicit permission first. 4.7 says chatbots must follow all guidelines. Apple has no rule written specifically for AI medical advice. — [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Starting 26 March 2026, Apple shows on US product pages whether an app is a regulated medical device. Some apps in the Health & Fitness or Medical categories, or apps with frequent medical references, must declare their medical-device status (US, UK, Europe reported). — [9to5Mac, 26 Mar 2026](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/26/new-app-store-policy-requires-medical-device-disclosures-for-some-health-apps/); [Becker's Hospital Review](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/digital-health/apple-adds-medical-device-labels-for-us-health-apps/) (search-snippet level only; full article not fetched)
- Apple updated its guidelines in June 2026 with stricter rules on low-quality apps. — [MacRumors, 9 Jun 2026](https://macrumors.com/2026/06/09/app-store-guidelines-low-quality-apps/)

**Documented rejections and removals (developer-reported)**
- The search found no report of a peptide tracker being removed outright from either store. The documented pattern is developers **removing dose or reconstitution calculators** to stay listed. One developer wrote that App Store rules only allow dose calculators from approved sources, "so we've taken it out to keep Peptide Tracker available". Pep AI made the same change. Another developer said "Apple's App Review Guideline 1.4.2 required us to remove it in version 1.5.5". VialWise removed its vial-label scanner, syringe view and vial reminders and became "a tracker and a cited reference library". Peptide Library removed all calculators from its iPhone app. — [VialWise App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/vialwise-peptide-tracker/id6774017323); [Pep AI App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/pep-ai-peptide-glp-1-tracker/id6758682710); [Peptide Library App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/peptide-library-and-tracker/id6755092737); [LynkDose comparison of 24 apps](https://lynkdose.com/blog/best-peptide-tracker-app/); [Peptide Library comparison](https://peptidelibrary.app/compare/best-peptide-tracker-apps) (search summaries; the store pages were not individually fetched)
- On Android, Peptide Library, Peptide Tracker, PeptidePal, Pep AI, Shotsy and PeptIQ are reported as available on Google Play. No Google Play enforcement against these apps was found. — [Peptide Library comparison](https://peptidelibrary.app/compare/best-peptide-tracker-apps)
- AI health chat apps have been rejected repeatedly under 1.4.1. In a November 2025 forum thread, the developer of an AI veterinary chat app that gave diagnoses and treatment suggestions said it was still rejected after adding a pre-use disclaimer pop-up, a disclaimer on every AI reply, and an algorithm registry filing. Apple's only reply suggested a one-on-one App Review consultation. — [Apple Developer Forums thread 807889](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/807889); see also [thread 772732](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/772732) and [thread 807508 ("wellness-only but still flagged as medical")](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/807508)

**Google Play (search summaries of support.google.com pages; not fetched directly)**
- Google bans apps that promote or sell **unapproved substances** "regardless of any legality claims", supplements with dangerous ingredients, and products with false health claims. — [Google Play policy page](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en)
- Every app must complete the Health apps declaration in Play Console, including apps on testing tracks. Accurate declarations have been required since 31 Aug 2024. — [Google Play Health apps declaration](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14738291)
- Apps with medical functionality need proof of regulatory approval or a disclaimer. Non-device health apps need a disclaimer that they are not medical devices and do not diagnose, treat, cure or prevent conditions. Oximetry claims based only on the app are banned. Google is aligning with EU MDCG guidance and adding a "Medical Device" label for EU-certified apps. — [Google Play Health Content and Services](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en); [policy preview](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13996823)
- The Health and Medical Apps policy has been reported effective **28 Jan 2026**. It bans "misleading or potentially harmful" health functionality and covers any app that accesses health data. — [Google Play support](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16555673)

### Inferences
- Shipping a peptide reconstitution or dose calculator from an unaffiliated startup is likely to be rejected under Apple 1.4.2. The safer design is (a) no calculator, (b) displaying only the dose a clinician entered, as a log entry, or (c) a calculator released under a pharmacy, hospital or university partner's developer account, or with regulator approval.
- Peptides that are unapproved or sold as "research use only" (BPC-157, TB-500, retatrutide and similar) create risk on Google Play ("unapproved substances") and Apple (1.4.3 and 1.4.1) if the app links to vendors, shows prices, or encourages use. A neutral logging tool with no vendor links or affiliate codes is lower risk.
- Apple may treat an AI chatbot that answers dosing or symptom questions as "medical advice", and adding disclaimers alone did not get the reported apps approved. The chat should be limited to explaining sourced reference content, or should route users to a clinician.
- The app should be published by a registered company, not an individual (5.1.1(ix)). Plan for Apple's medical-device declaration and Google's health declaration from the start.

### Gaps
- No Apple or Google statement was found that names a peptide app rejection. All evidence comes from developers.
- The exact current Google Play text on unapproved substances (its example list) could not be fetched. Whether Google lists specific peptides by name is unknown.
- No report was found of a peptide **vendor** (e-commerce) app being removed in 2024–2026.

## 2. US FDA and FTC actions on peptides and compounded GLP-1s (2023–Nov 2026)

### Takeaway
Since 2025 the FDA has (1) declared the tirzepatide and semaglutide shortages over and ended enforcement discretion for mass compounding, (2) sent several waves of warning letters to telehealth sellers about compounded GLP-1 claims, and (3) in 2026 shut the "research use only" loophole with batches of warning letters to peptide websites. BPC-157's status is changing. It was placed on 503A Category 2 in Sept 2023 and reportedly removed from Category 2 in April 2026. A July 2026 advisory committee voted 8–6 to recommend it for the 503A bulks list, but no final rule exists, so compounding it is still not clearly lawful. The FTC has acted against GLP-1 telehealth marketing (NextMed).

### Cited Findings
**BPC-157 and other peptides on Category 2**
- BPC-157 was placed in Category 2 ("substances with safety concerns") in September 2023. Sources dated 2025 say this barred compounding pharmacies from using it. — [DJ Holt Law](https://djholtlaw.com/deep-dive-regulatory-status-of-popular-compounded-peptides/); [Wikipedia: BPC-157](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BPC-157)
- Sources conflict on 2026 status. Pharmacy and industry sources say that in April 2026 the FDA removed BPC-157, TB-500 and CJC-1295 (a reported 12 peptides in total) from Category 2 after their nominations were withdrawn. One source gives 15 April and another 22 April. A guide dated Oct 2026 says FDA's page still listed BPC-157 and TB-500 in Category 2 when it checked. — [New Drug Loft / VLS Pharmacy](https://newdrugloft.com/recent-regulatory-updates-on-compounded-peptide-injections/); [Centre for Medical Humanities guide](https://centreformedicalhumanities.org/fda-category-2-bulk-drug-substances-list-guide/); [DJ Holt Law 2026 alert](https://djholtlaw.com/regulatory-alert-the-legal-status-of-bpc-157-in-compounding-and-clinical-practice/)
- The FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee met 23–24 July 2026. It reportedly voted 8–6 (1 abstention) to recommend BPC-157 for the 503A bulks list for a proposed ulcerative colitis indication. FDA reviewers had reportedly recommended against. The vote is advisory only, and commentators expect rulemaking to run into 2027 or later. Removal from Category 2 "do[es] not represent current legal authority to compound". — [FDA PCAC meeting page](https://www.fda.gov/advisory-committees/advisory-committee-calendar/july-23-24-2026-meeting-pharmacy-compounding-advisory-committee-07232026); [DJ Holt Law](https://djholtlaw.com/regulatory-alert-the-legal-status-of-bpc-157-in-compounding-and-clinical-practice/); [Spartacus Law Firm](https://spartacuslawfirm.com/can-you-still-compound-bpc-157-in-2026/)
- One unverified secondary source says HHS Secretary Kennedy announced in Feb 2026 a plan to move BPC-157 to Category 1. No primary confirmation was found. — [Centre for Medical Humanities guide](https://centreformedicalhumanities.org/fda-category-2-bulk-drug-substances-list-guide/)
- BPC-157 is not approved by any drug regulator for human use. — [Wikipedia: BPC-157](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BPC-157)

**End of the compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide shortages**
- The tirzepatide shortage was declared resolved first (fall 2024). Semaglutide followed about two months later. 503A and 503B compounders had to stop tirzepatide at once, and 503B facilities had until 22 May 2025 to stop compounding semaglutide. — [Science-Based Medicine](https://sciencebasedmedicine.org/the-fda-vs-compounding-pharmacies-glp-1-edition/); [Health Law Alliance](https://www.healthlawalliance.com/blog/compounded-glp-1-marketing-after-the-shortage-ended)
- The FDA proposed removing semaglutide, tirzepatide and liraglutide from the 503B bulks list. Sources date this to 30 April or 1 May 2026, and no finalization was found. FDA says only the base peptide forms are acceptable and salt forms (for example acetate) are unapproved. — [Pharmacy Times](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/fda-and-novo-nordisk-warned-of-glp-1-telehealth-compounding-take-down-what-s-next-); [GLP One Rx summary](https://glponerx.com/journal/fda-glp1-warning-letters-enforcement-2026) (secondary)
- The FDA reportedly had about 990 adverse event reports for compounded semaglutide and more than 730 for compounded tirzepatide "as of May 31" (year unclear in snippet). — [FDA concerns page via search summary](https://www.pharmtech.com/view/why-the-fda-issued-30-warning-letters-to-telehealth-sites-marketing-compounded-glp-1s)

**Warning letters to telehealth companies (compounded GLP-1 claims)**
- Waves of warning letters: more than 55 in Sept 2025, a reported batch of 30 in March 2026, and a reported third wave on 8 June 2026. They focus on claims that compounded GLP-1s are FDA-approved, the same as Wegovy or Zepbound, or made by the telehealth firm itself. — [PharmTech: 30 letters](https://www.pharmtech.com/view/why-the-fda-issued-30-warning-letters-to-telehealth-sites-marketing-compounded-glp-1s); [Yahoo/Reuters: 25 letters](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/us-fda-sends-25-letters-222507219.html); [Medical Daily](https://www.medicaldaily.com/fda-warning-letters-compounded-glp1-telehealth-claims-481400); [iMedic](https://imedic.health/en/health/news/fda-crackdown-compounded-glp1-drugs-telehealth-warning-letters-2026) (counts and dates vary across sources)

**Warning letters to "research use only" peptide sellers (2026)**
- On 7 April 2026 the FDA published seven CDER warning letters, dated 31 March 2026, to peptide websites selling GLP-1 analogs (retatrutide, tirzepatide and others): Gram Peptides, Pink Pony Peptides, Mile High Compounds, Prime Sciences, PekCura Labs, FormPour and Guangzhou Huli Technology. — [PolicyCanary](https://policycanary.io/blog/fda-research-peptide-warning-letters-april-2026); [ChemVerify](https://www.chemverify.com/learn/fda-research-use-only-enforcement-warning-letters-2026); [GLP-1 News Now](https://glp1newsnow.substack.com/p/not-for-human-use-grey-market-research)
- Five letters dated 24 Aug 2026 and posted 1 Sept 2026 went to Peak Performance Peptides, Royal Peptides, NuScience Peptides, Peptide Partners and TXP Innovations (Tex Peptides). The products cited include semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, SS-31, tesamorelin and PT-141, treated as unapproved new drugs under FD&C Act §505(a). The FDA said "research use only" labeling did not control, because drug claims on the websites and the sale of **bacteriostatic water for reconstitution** showed intended use as injectable human drugs. — [Biotech Research (BSR)](https://biotechresearch.co.uk/intelligence/fdas-september-2026-peptide-vendor-sweep-five-warning-letters-one-clear-doctrine-on-research-use-only-claims); [GLP-1 US](https://glp1-us.com/news/2026-08-24-fda-warns-research-peptide-sellers/); [Peptide Review Desk](https://www.peptidereviewdesk.com/research/tracker/2026-08-research-peptide-seller-warning-letters/)
- One tracker counts 175 FDA warning letters in 2026 through May, with 43 involving GLP-1s, peptides or compounded semaglutide/tirzepatide. This is the tracker's own count. — [VialAudit](https://vialaudit.com/articles/fda-peptide-enforcement-2024-2026); [White Label Clinic tracker](https://www.whitelabelclinic.com/compliance/fda-glp-1-enforcement-tracker)
- Reports say the FDA is using AI to flag websites of "research use only" peptide vendors. — [LetsTalkPeps](https://www.letstalkpeps.com/articles/fda-ai-peptide-vendors) (low-authority source)

**FTC**
- In July 2025 the FTC sued NextMed (telehealth GLP-1 programme) and two of its principals. The complaint cited unsubstantiated weight-loss claims ("lost on average 53 pounds"), fake Trustpilot reviews, testimonials and before/after photos of non-customers, hidden drug costs, and difficult cancellation. The final order requires $150,000 in refunds, competent evidence for weight-loss claims, and clear disclosure of cancellation and refund terms. — [Federal Register, 17 Jul 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-07-17/html/2025-13407.htm); [Foley & Lardner, Aug 2025](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2025/08/health-care-marketing-enforcement-ftc-targets-telehealth-company-for-deceptive-w/); [Fox Rothschild, Jan 2026](https://advertisinglaw.foxrothschild.com/2026/01/when-too-good-to-be-true-really-is-too-good-to-be-true-ftc-settles-with-nextmed-regarding-deceptive-advertising-practices/); [ConsumerAffairs](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/feds-finalize-action-against-nextmed-for-glp-1-sales-practices-120525.html); [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/ftc-uses-its-consumer-protection-powers-to-regulate-sellers-of-glp-1s)

### Inferences
- The FDA's "intended use" doctrine (website claims plus sale of reconstitution supplies) means an app that pairs peptide content with vendor links, discount codes or reconstitution guidance could be cited as evidence of intended human use, for the app and for any partner vendor. A safety-first app should not monetize through peptide vendors.
- The app should not describe compounded GLP-1s as "the same as" Ozempic, Wegovy or Zepbound, or as approved. The app should not publish unsubstantiated outcome numbers or curated testimonials (FTC NextMed).
- BPC-157's status changed during 2026 and may change again. In-app content should state each peptide's regulatory status with a date and source, rather than calling it "legal" or "FDA-cleared".

### Gaps
- FDA primary pages could not be fetched. The Category 2 table, the date of the shortage declarations and the AE counts were not checked against fda.gov.
- No primary confirmation of any HHS or Kennedy announcement on peptides was found.
- No FTC action specific to peptides (as opposed to GLP-1 telehealth) was found.

## 3. When a tracker, calculator or triage app becomes a medical device (SaMD), and how African regulators treat it

### Takeaway
Under US, EU and IMDRF frameworks, logging, reminders and general wellness are low risk or receive enforcement discretion. Software that **calculates a patient-specific drug dose**, or that **triages symptoms or suggests diagnosis or treatment**, is generally a medical device. Under EU MDR Rule 11, dose-calculation software is commentators' standard example of Class IIb to III. In Africa, Nigeria (NAFDAC, July 2024) and Kenya (PPB, 2026) now have specific SaMD registration guidelines. South Africa has general device classification rules plus a 2025 AI-device communication. Zimbabwe's MCAZ apparently does not yet regulate most devices, software included: device regulations were still in draft as of 2023, so this is a gap, not a safe harbour.

### Cited Findings
**US FDA**
- The FDA's "Policy for Device Software Functions and Mobile Medical Applications" (2013, updated 2015, 2019 and 2022) lists software it treats under enforcement discretion in Appendix B (for example patient self-management and logging). Appendix C covers functions that are actively regulated. — [FDA Device Software Functions page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health/mobile-medical-applications); [FDA guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/80958/download)
- On 6 Jan 2026 the FDA issued updated final General Wellness and Clinical Decision Support (CDS) guidances without prior public comment. The wellness guidance keeps the two-factor test and adds a wrist-worn blood-pressure wearable example: wellness status now turns on how the product is promoted. The CDS guidance reinterprets Criterion 3, no longer excludes time-critical support per se, and drops the 2022 "may exhibit signs" statement. Neither guidance expressly changes FDA's approach to AI. — [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/it/insights/2026/01/a-relaxing-2026-fda-updates-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance); [Faegre Drinker](https://faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/1/key-updates-in-fdas-2026-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance); [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/01/fda-cuts-red-tape-on-clinical-decision-support-software); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-br/insights/publications/2026/01/fda-updates-its-clinical-decision-support-and-general-wellness-guidances-key-points); [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/fda-adapts-with-the-times-on-digital-health-updated-guidances-on-general-wellness-products)
- Apps that control insulin delivery on a pump are treated as accessories to regulated devices. — [Mintz, 2017](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2017-10-building-health-app-part-3-what-you-need-know-about-fdas)

**EU MDR Rule 11, MDCG 2019-11 and IMDRF**
- MDCG 2019-11 adopts the IMDRF SaMD risk framework: classification depends on how significant the software's information is to the healthcare decision and how serious the patient's condition is. — [NAMSA](https://namsa.com/resources/blog/eu-mdr-and-ivdr-classifying-medical-device-software-mdsw/); [Bristows](https://www.bristows.com/news/deciphering-rule-11-new-guidance-on-the-classification-of-software-medical-devices-under-the-eu-mdr); [EC MDCG 2019-11](https://ec.europa.eu/docsroom/documents/37581/attachments/1/translations/en/renditions/native)
- Commentators say software "used to calculate dosages of drugs, to select drugs… will probably all fall within class III". The Johner Institute cites a cytostatic dose-calculation app that moved from Class I under the MDD to Class III under the MDR. — [Simmons & Simmons](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/ck1t1brmb5c820b235aka2g0e/software-under-the-medical-devices-regulations); [Johner Institute](https://www.johner-institute.com/articles/regulatory-affairs/mdr-rule-11-software/)

**Africa**
- **Nigeria (NAFDAC):** Guidelines for Registration of Software as a Medical Device, ref. DR&R-GDL-036-00, effective 1 July 2024. No medical device may be "manufactured, imported, exported, advertised, sold, distributed, or used in Nigeria" unless registered. The guideline uses IMDRF definitions and IEC 62304. — [NAFDAC SaMD Guideline PDF](https://nafdac.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/Files/Resources/Guidelines/DR_And_R_Guidelines/Guidelines-for-Registration-of-Software-as-a-Medical-Device-SaMD-in-Nigeria.pdf); [Mondaq](https://webiis08.mondaq.com/nigeria/healthcare/1629510/registration-of-software-as-a-medical-device-with-nafdac-a-comprehensive-guide)
- **Kenya (PPB):** Guideline on Regulation of Medical Device Software (MDSW), 2026. It splits software into SaMD and SiMD, requires evidence of safety and accuracy before market entry, and requires post-market monitoring across the lifecycle. It is IMDRF-aligned. Reported publication dates differ (February vs April 2026). — [The Star (Kenya), 22 Apr 2026](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2026-04-22-new-rules-set-for-health-apps-medical-software); [Standard Media](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/business/opinion/article/2001551793/what-ai-bill-and-ppb-software-device-rules-mean-for-healthcare-businesses); [Techweez, May 2026](https://techweez.com/2026/05/06/kenya-ai-healthcare-regulations-ppb-framework/); [Health Business Kenya](https://healthbusiness.co.ke/10137/kenya-tightens-oversight-of-medical-device-software/)
- **South Africa (SAHPRA):** devices are classified under the Medical Device Regulations of Act 101 of 1965 per guideline SAHPGL-MD-04 v5 (27 Feb 2025). Communication MD08 (2025/2026) on AI medical devices states that AI/ML-enabled devices can be SaMD. A clinical evaluation guideline (MD-16, 8 Sept 2025) and a draft reliance guideline (MD-19, Nov 2025) are also in place. No SAHPRA guideline dedicated to SaMD registration was found. — [SAHPRA classification guideline](https://www.sahpra.org.za/document/classification-of-medical-devices-and-ivds/); [SAHPRA MD08 AI devices](https://www.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/MD08-20252026_-SAHPRA-Communication-to-Industry-AI-Medical-devices_Acknowledgements.pdf); [SAHPGL-MD-16](https://www.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/SAHPGL-MD-16_v1-Guideline-on-Clinical-Evaluation-of-Medical-Devices.pdf); [SAHPGL-MD-19 draft](https://www.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/SAHGL-MD-19_v1-MEDICAL-DEVICES-RELIANCE-GUIDELINE-document-for-comment.pdf); [SAHPRA borderline products guideline](https://www.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/SAHPGL-CRO-01_v1-Guideline-for-Borderline-Products-1.pdf)
- **Zimbabwe (MCAZ):** only condoms (SI 183 of 2005) and gloves (SI 1 of 2016) are clearly regulated as devices. MCAZ staff described the device regulations as "still in draft stage and not yet approved/gazetted" (2023 study). A consultancy claims regulation under SI 150 of 2018, which could not be verified. No MCAZ software or SaMD guidance was found. — [Pharma Legal Handbook: Zimbabwe](https://pharmaboardroom.com/legal-reports/the-pharma-legal-handbook-zimbabwe/); [PLOS One, 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0287495); [medRxiv qualitative study](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/medrxiv/early/2023/06/12/2023.06.07.23291092.full.pdf); [Nexor (consultancy claim)](https://nexortest.com/global-registration/mcaz-medical-device-registration-zimbabwe/)
- Ghana FDA also publishes a SaMD registration guideline. — [Ghana FDA](https://fdaghana.gov.gh/guideline-for-registration-of-software-as-medical-device)

### Inferences
- **Lower-risk design:** log doses the user enters (as prescribed), reminders, inventory and expiry tracking, symptom and side-effect diaries, education with citations, and a clinician-shareable PDF. These fit general wellness or patient self-management.
- **Device territory:** an in-app reconstitution or "units to draw" calculator; dose titration or escalation suggestions; symptom triage that outputs "seek care / continue / reduce dose"; and an AI chatbot that answers personal dosing questions. These are likely SaMD under IMDRF, EU Rule 11, NAFDAC and Kenya PPB. Expansion into Nigeria or Kenya with such features would need registration.
- Zimbabwe's lack of software regulation is not a safe harbour. MCAZ could gazette device regulations later. App-store rules (Apple 1.4.2) apply worldwide whatever local law says. For expansion, designing to IMDRF and NAFDAC/PPB norms is the safer default.
- If the app ever adds rPPG camera vitals, Apple 1.4.1 bans sensor-only claims for blood pressure, SpO2 and glucose without validation, and Google bans app-only oximetry.

### Gaps
- The exact text of FDA Appendix B/C examples on medication dose calculators was not retrieved (fda.gov blocked).
- The full text and effective date of the Kenya PPB MDSW guideline were not verified from ppb.go.ke.
- No MCAZ statement on health apps was found. Whether the 1991 MASCA regulations' definitions could cover software is unknown. A Zimbabwean regulatory lawyer should be consulted.

## 4. Liability: dosing errors with compounded GLP-1 vials, and how apps disclaim

### Takeaway
The best-documented harm is the FDA's July 2024 alert. Patients drawing from multi-dose vials of compounded semaglutide took **5 to 20 times** the intended dose, due to confusion between mg, mL and "units" and to prescriber conversion errors. Some needed hospital care. No lawsuits over these dosing errors were found. A unit-conversion feature is the most hazardous possible feature, and disclaimers alone did not satisfy Apple in the reported cases.

### Cited Findings
- FDA (July 2024): it received reports of adverse events, some requiring hospitalization, tied to dosing errors with compounded semaglutide in multi-dose vials. Most involved patients drawing up too much. Doses were "five to 20 times more than the intended dose". Contributing factors: unfamiliarity with vial and syringe use; confusion among mL, mg and "units"; varying concentrations of compounded products; and prescriber conversion errors. In one example a clinician meant 0.25 mg (5 units) but wrote 25 units, and the patient had severe vomiting. Effects included GI symptoms, fainting, headache, dehydration, acute pancreatitis and gallstones. The long half-life (about one week) can require prolonged observation. — [FDA alert on dosing errors](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/fda-alerts-health-care-providers-compounders-and-patients-dosing-errors-associated-compounded); [CBS News](https://cbsnews.com/news/weight-loss-semaglutide-fda-wegovy); [Drugs.com](https://drugs.com/news/fda-warns-accidental-overdoses-compounded-versions-ozempic-120510.html); [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/compounded-semaglutide-overdoses-tied-hospitalizations)
- One reported patient searched online for dosing advice after receiving unclear instructions from a telemedicine prescriber, and then took five times the intended dose. — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/weight-loss-semaglutide-fda-wegovy); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/people-are-overdosing-on-semaglutide-drugs-like-ozempic-and-wegovy/)
- Disclaimer practice: Apple 1.4.1 expects apps to remind users to consult a doctor. Google requires non-device health apps to state they are not medical devices and do not diagnose or treat. One developer's per-reply AI disclaimers and consent pop-up did not prevent rejection. — [Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [Google Play health policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en); [Apple Forums 807889](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/807889)

### Inferences
- If the app shows any unit or volume, it should display mg, mL and syringe units together, require the dose to come from the prescriber or pharmacy label, flag entries far outside the label range (for example more than 2× the last logged dose) as "check with your prescriber" without suggesting an alternative, and never convert concentrations for the user. Even this "sanity check" may be read as dose-related CDS, so it should be reviewed against Apple 1.4.2.
- A single serious overdose linked to the app could cause both store removal and reputational and legal exposure. Product liability for apps in Zimbabwe and South Africa is untested (no cases found), but South Africa's Consumer Protection Act strict-liability regime is a plausible route. This was not researched in depth.

### Gaps
- No lawsuits or court filings over compounded GLP-1 dosing errors, or over app-mediated peptide dosing harm, were found.
- No updated FDA figures on dosing errors after 2024 were found.
- Product-liability and consumer-protection exposure for apps in Zimbabwe and South Africa was not researched.

## 5. Advertising rules for prescription medicines in Zimbabwe and South Africa that affect app marketing

### Takeaway
Both countries restrict medicine advertising. In **Zimbabwe**, advertising **any** medicine without MCAZ's prior written approval is a criminal offence (MASCA s.40 and SI 150 of 1991 s.65), and in 2024–2026 MCAZ has targeted social-media influencers. In **South Africa**, Schedule 2+ medicines (semaglutide is S4) may be advertised only to health professionals (Medicines Act s.18, Reg 42). SAHPRA and the SAPC stepped up enforcement against unregistered compounded GLP-1s in May 2026. App marketing that names or promotes specific GLP-1 or peptide products to consumers is therefore high risk in both markets.

### Cited Findings
**Zimbabwe**
- MCAZ says the Medicines and Allied Substances Control Act (Ch. 15:03) bars anyone from advertising a medicine without prior written approval from the Authority. It cites s.40 of the Act and s.65 of the 1991 General Regulations (SI 150 of 1991, s.65(1)(b) and (3)). The Act's definition of "sell" includes advertising. — [Pindula, 13 Feb 2024](https://news.pindula.co.zw/2024/02/13/advertising-medicines-without-written-approval-from-mcaz-a-criminal-offence); [The Herald: MCAZ warns media houses](https://www.herald.co.zw/mcaz-warns-media-houses/); [MASCA text (UNODC SHERLOC)](https://sherloc.unodc.org/cld/uploads/res/document/zwe/medicines-and-allied-substances-control-act_html/Zimbabwe_Medicines_and_Allied_Substances_Control_Act.pdf); [ZimLII Act](https://zimlii.org/akn/zw/act/1969/14)
- MCAZ: advertising prescription medicines directly to the public, unrealistic claims, and "miracle cure" promotion are "not only unethical but punishable by law". MCAZ works with the Zimbabwe Republic Police to prosecute. — [AllAfrica, Apr 2025](https://allafrica.com/stories/202504100497.html); [NewsDay](https://newsday.co.zw/local-news/article/200006394/mcaz-issues-warning-to-people-adversting-illegal-medicines)
- In July 2026 MCAZ began engaging influencers and content creators over illegal medicine promotion on social media, citing a rise in influencer ads. — [AllAfrica, 16 Jul 2026](https://allafrica.com/stories/202607160534.html); [Herald: MCAZ engages content creators](https://www.heraldonline.co.zw/mcaz-engages-content-creators-over-medicine-ads/); [Herald: MCAZ warns celebrities](https://www.heraldonline.co.zw/mcaz-warns-celebrities-about-advertising-medicines-without-its-permission/)
- Zimbabwe has Guidelines on Advertising and Promotion of Medicines (indexed on FAOLEX/LEAP). — [UNEP LEAP / FAOLEX](https://leap.unep.org/en/countries/zw/national-legislation/guidelines-advertising-and-promotion-medicines-zimbabwe)
- Background critique of Zimbabwe's advertising rules and patient safety. — [BMJ blog, 2017](https://blogs.bmj.com/bmj/2017/06/08/dudzai-mureyi-et-al-drug-advertising-rules-and-the-patient-safety-paradox-in-zimbabwe/)

**South Africa**
- Medicines Act s.18: no person may advertise a medicine, scheduled substance, medical device or IVD unless the advertisement complies with prescribed requirements. Under the General Regulations (Reg 42), Schedule 2–6 medicines may be advertised only to pharmacists, medical practitioners, dentists and similar professionals. Advertising S2+ products to the public is a SAHPRA enforcement focus. Contraventions are offences under s.29 (fines or imprisonment). — [CMS Expert Guide: South Africa](https://cms.law/en/int/expert-guides/cms-expert-guide-to-advertising-of-medicines-and-medical-devices/south-africa); [Acts Online Reg 42](https://www.acts.co.za/medicines-/n859_42__advertising_of_medici); [SAHPRA advertising monitoring form](https://www.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/OF-RC-INSP-06D_v1-Report-for-Monitoring-and-Advertising-of-Promotional-Material.pdf); [BusinessLive, Mar 2024](https://www.businesslive.co.za/bd/opinion/editorials/2024-03-06-letter-doctors-must-stick-to-the-label/)
- SAHPRA (Dec 2023): Ozempic is not registered in South Africa for weight loss. Salt forms of semaglutide are not permissible under the Medicines Act. — [SAHPRA media release, 11 Dec 2023](https://www.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/MEDIA-RELEASE-Public-urged-to-use-registered-Ozempic-semaglutide-products-11-December-2023.pdf); [SAHPRA position on compounded semaglutide, 13 Dec 2023](https://sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/MEDIA-RELEASE-SAHPRA-position-on-Semaglutide-compounded-products-13-December-2023.pdf)
- SAHPRA and the SA Pharmacy Council (23 May 2026) stepped up enforcement against unlawful manufacture and distribution of unregistered GLP-1/GIP medicines promoted for weight loss. Compounding "cannot be used as a mechanism for large-scale manufacture, advertising, or distribution of unregistered medicines." — [SAHPRA press release](https://www.sahpra.org.za/press-releases/sahpra-and-the-sapc-crack-down-on-unlawful-manufacturing-of-unregistered-glp-1-medicines/)

### Inferences
- App store listings, social ads and influencer campaigns should promote the **tool** (tracking, safety, reminders, side-effect diary), not named medicines or peptides. Screenshots and ASO keywords should not feature brand names such as "Ozempic" or "BPC-157" as benefits. Generic logging fields can list them neutrally.
- Paid influencer content showing a specific GLP-1 or peptide in use could be treated as unapproved medicine advertising by MCAZ, and as S2+ public advertising by SAHPRA.
- Unregistered peptides (BPC-157 and similar) and compounded or salt-form semaglutide are unregistered or unlawful in South Africa. Content or features that help users obtain them (vendor directories, price comparisons) carry high regulatory risk in addition to the app-store risk.

### Gaps
- The exact current text of MASCA s.40 and SI 150/1991 s.65 was not checked against the official gazette. Whether an app that displays medicine names counts as "advertising" under MASCA is untested.
- Whether MCAZ has acted specifically on GLP-1 or peptide products (as SAHPRA has) was not found.
- The South African Marketing Code (MCA) and Advertising Regulatory Board rulings on health apps were not researched.
- No research was done on Kenya and Nigeria advertising rules (NAFDAC requires registration before a device can be "advertised").
